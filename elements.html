How Our Models Work

A high-level overview of the ideas and calculations behind each live model.

Hidden Markov Model

A market regime cannot be observed directly, so the model treats it as a hidden state and uses observable price behavior to estimate which state is most likely. Its initial-state probabilities describe where the process may begin, its transition matrix describes how likely one regime is to follow another, and its emission matrix connects each hidden regime to the daily return patterns it tends to produce.

The forward and backward algorithms calculate state probabilities across the observed history. Baum-Welch learning then uses those probabilities to improve the model's initial, transition, and emission estimates. Once trained, the model normalizes the latest state probabilities and can project them forward through repeated applications of the transition matrix.

Black-Litterman Model

The model begins with a neutral market baseline instead of relying only on unstable historical return estimates. It combines market-capitalization weights, the covariance matrix, and a risk-aversion coefficient to infer the equilibrium returns that would make the current market portfolio reasonable.

Investor views are then encoded as expected relative or absolute returns, along with an uncertainty level for each view. Black-Litterman blends those views with the market baseline using Bayesian weighting, so confident views have more influence and uncertain views stay closer to equilibrium. The resulting posterior expected returns feed into portfolio optimization to produce diversified asset weights.

Heston Stochastic Volatility

The Heston model allows variance to change over time instead of assuming one constant volatility. Variance is pulled toward a long-run level at a chosen mean-reversion speed while receiving random shocks scaled by its current level. A separate correlation parameter links shocks in the asset price to shocks in variance, capturing the relationship between market moves and changing volatility.

To turn those dynamics into option prices, the model expresses the future log-price distribution through a characteristic function that satisfies the Heston pricing equation. The Carr-Madan transformation makes the pricing problem suitable for computation, and an inverse Fast Fourier Transform evaluates many strikes efficiently. Repeating that calculation across strikes and maturities produces the implied volatility surface.

Prop Firm Strategy Simulator

The simulator treats an account as a sequence of states: evaluation, funded, reset, payout, or failure. It reads completed trades from separate built-in evaluation and funded logs, counts only each trade's exit row, and updates account equity with its realized profit or loss. During evaluation it applies the selected profit target, daily-loss limit, trailing drawdown, minimum trading days, and best-day consistency rule. A pass starts a fresh funded account; an evaluation failure charges the selected reset fee.

In the funded state, the replay applies the funded daily and maximum-loss limits and checks whether the selected winning-day and payout thresholds have been met. The historical result is total simulated payouts minus evaluation reset fees. The chosen 1-, 2-, or 3-year window ends at the latest timestamp in the included trade data.

The Monte Carlo comparison measures path sensitivity. Shuffling without replacement uses every observed trade outcome exactly once but changes its order, isolating sequence risk. Bootstrap resampling draws outcomes with replacement, so individual trades may recur or disappear and both order and composition change. The distribution of net results summarizes these randomized paths; it is conditional on the supplied logs and rules and is not a forecast.
